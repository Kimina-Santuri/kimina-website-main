Santuri DNA: Music Industry and Culture

How can artists take part in musical exchange while protecting context, credit and community?

Work through these six tasks with a partner. You only need a notebook, a pen and your voice. Total time is about 3 hours.

Download the printable PDF

Before you start

Decide who is Partner A and who is Partner B. You swap roles in every task, so both of you talk and both of you listen.

  • Talker: explains, answers, thinks out loud.
  • Asker: asks the questions in the task, and keeps asking "How do you know that?" and "Can you give an example?"

Pair rules

  1. Give an example before an opinion.
  2. "I assumed…" is a strong thing to say, not a weak one.
  3. Write your partner's best idea down, not just your own.
  4. Disagree with the idea, then build on it.

Listen and AskPeople, place, purpose, tools.

Pick two or three short sounds, or use your own examples: a song from home, a sound from your neighbourhood, a track you love, something you've made. For each one, take turns asking each other these four questions. Write one answer for each in your notebook.

  1. People: Who made this first? Who makes it now?
  2. Place: Where was it made? What kind of event or room was it for?
  3. Purpose: Was it for dancing, mourning, praying, working, celebrating?
  4. Tools: What shaped the sound? A hand-built instrument, a cheap phone, a big sound system, software on a borrowed laptop?

Example

Singeli from Dar es Salaam. People: young producers and MCs. Place: street parties. Purpose: dancing, very fast. Tools: cheap software and loud sound systems.

Talk about it together · 5 min

If this sound turned up in a track made in London or Berlin tomorrow, what should travel with it? Write one sentence you both agree on:

"When a sound moves, it should take with it…"

Influence MapWhat you know, what you assume, what to check.

On your own (8 min). Write your project name in the middle of a page. Draw three branches. On each, write one influence: an artist, a scene, a teacher, a technique, a place or a tool. Under each, write:

  • I know: something you can point to.
  • I assume: something you believe but haven't checked.
  • To check: who or what could confirm it.

Consent and Credit CasesDecide together: permission, credit, documentation.

First, learn four words. Use them to name what's happening in each case.

WordWhat it means
InspirationYou take an idea or a feeling, not the recording or the exact notes.
CollaborationYou make it together and share the credit (and any money).
SamplingYou use part of someone else's recording. It needs permission.
AppropriationYou take without context, credit or consent, often from people with less power.

How to work. Take the cases in turns: A reads one aloud, B answers first, then A adds or disagrees. Agree together and write one line per case. Do at least four cases.

"This is ______. We need permission from ______. We should credit ______. We should write down ______."

A. The river song

You record a woman singing while washing clothes by the river in your home village. You want her voice as the hook. She said "yes, record", but didn't know it would be released. Is that consent?

B. The old benga record

You sample 4 bars from a 1970s Kenyan benga record you found at a market. The band is no longer around. Who do you ask? What if you can't find anyone?

C. One take, no contract

A friend plays nyatiti on your track in one take, "just for vibes". Six months later the track is popular. What did they agree to on the day?

D. The sample pack

A producer in Europe offers good money for "authentic African field recordings" for a sample pack. Who is in those recordings, and will they ever know?

E. The viral remix

You remix a classmate's track from this course without asking and post it on TikTok. It goes viral. Does being classmates change anything?

F. The sacred chant

Your track uses a chant from a ceremony. An elder says it should never be played in clubs. Does the community's rule matter even if the law allows it?

Hint. A recording has two owners: whoever owns the song (words and melody, called publishing) and whoever owns the recording itself (the master). A sample usually needs a yes from both.

Follow the ReleaseWho owns it, who gets paid, who gets forgotten.

The track

"Mvua" by a Nairobi student. The singer wrote the lyrics and melody. A producer made the beat. It uses a kayamba loop recorded in Kilifi, with permission. It's released on Spotify, Boomplay and YouTube through a distributor, played on radio, and used in a TikTok trend.

RoleWhat they do
SongwriterWrites the words and melody. Owns the song (publishing) unless they sign it away.
PerformerSings or plays on the recording.
ProducerMakes the beat and shapes the sound.
Master ownerPaid for the recording, so owns the recording itself.
Sample sourceThe person heard in the sample (here, the kayamba player).
PublisherHelps the songwriter register the song and collect songwriting money, for a share.
Collecting societyCollects money when music is played on radio, in clubs and in public, only for people registered with them.
DistributorUploads the track to platforms. Needs correct names in the metadata.
PlatformSpotify, Boomplay, YouTube, TikTok. Keeps a cut and pays the rest to whoever the metadata names.
ListenerStreams, shares, dances. Where the money starts.

Talk it through. Take turns: A asks the question, B answers, then swap for the next one.

  1. Who owns the song in "Mvua"? Who owns the recording?
  2. Who paid for the studio session? Why does that matter?
  3. What did the kayamba player agree to, and where is it written down?
  4. The radio plays "Mvua". Who gets paid, and what do they have to do first?
  5. The distributor's metadata only lists the singer. Who loses out?
  6. A TikTok trend uses the track. Who earns, and who is invisible?

Draw it · 5 min

Together, draw "Mvua" in the middle of a page and the roles around it. Draw arrows for where the money goes. Circle the role that's easiest to forget.

Interview Each OtherYour final project, from vague to clear.

Take a short private moment first. Write one line only you will see:

"Before today I assumed ______. Now I think ______, because ______."

Interview (10 min each). A interviews B using the seven questions below and writes B's answers in B's notebook. Then swap. If an answer sounds like the "vague" column, ask again.

Ethics Check, Log and CommitmentMake it clear, write it down, say it out loud.

Ethics check (15 min). A reads their paragraph aloud twice, slowly. B listens for these four things and asks about anything missing:

  • Credit: Is everyone in the project named?
  • Consent: Has each person said yes, and to what?
  • Context: Is it clear where the sounds come from?
  • Release: Is it clear where people will hear it and what they'll be told?

B then says "One thing missing is…" and "A clearer sentence would be…". A writes the suggestion down word for word. Swap.

Example

A: "I'll use some sounds from my grandmother."

B: "One thing missing is what she agreed to. A clearer sentence would be: 'I'll use two recordings of my grandmother singing, which she agreed to on 2 November, credited as Mama Achieng.'"
